21:T5a0f,<p>Think about the last time you used a calculator. You typed in a number, hit a button: +, -, ×, ÷ - typed another number, and got a result. Simple as that.</p>
<p>JavaScript operators work exactly the same way. They sit between two values, do something with them, and give you back a result. The only difference? JavaScript&#39;s calculator is a lot smarter. It doesn&#39;t just do math - it compares things, makes decisions, and remembers values.</p>
<blockquote>
<p>If you can use a calculator, you already understand JavaScript operators. You just need to learn its extra modes.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>In this post, we&#39;ll use a calculator as our mental model for every type of operator in JavaScript, including the one button, <code>==</code>, that trips up almost every beginner. By the end, none of this will feel foreign, because it isn&#39;t.</p>
<hr>
<h2 id="operators-are-the-calculators-buttons">Operators Are the Calculator&#39;s Buttons<a class="heading-anchor" href="#operators-are-the-calculators-buttons" aria-label="Link to this section">#</a></h2>
<p>Pick up a basic calculator. It has two things: numbers and buttons. The numbers are your raw data. The buttons — +, -, =, % — are your <strong>operators</strong>.</p>
<p>An operator is a symbol that tells JavaScript to perform a specific action on one or more values. Those values are called <strong>operands</strong>. Press <code>5 + 3</code> on a calculator and you get <code>8</code>. In JavaScript, it&#39;s exactly the same:</p>
<div class="code-block">
      <div class="code-head">
        <span class="code-lang">JavaScript</span>
        <button type="button" class="code-copy">Copy</button>
      </div>
      <pre class="shiki github-light-high-contrast" tabindex="0"><code><span class="line"><span style="color:#A0111F">let</span><span style="color:#0E1116"> result </span><span style="color:#A0111F">=</span><span style="color:#023B95"> 5</span><span style="color:#A0111F"> +</span><span style="color:#023B95"> 3</span><span style="color:#0E1116">;</span></span>
<span class="line"><span style="color:#0E1116">console.</span><span style="color:#622CBC">log</span><span style="color:#0E1116">(result); </span><span style="color:#66707B">// 8</span></span></code></pre>
    </div><blockquote>
<p>🧮 <strong>Calculator Analogy:</strong> The numbers on the screen are your values. The buttons you press are your operators. Without the buttons, the numbers just sit there doing nothing.</p>
</blockquote>
<hr>
<h2 id="arithmetic-is-standard-mode-">Arithmetic Is Standard Mode (+, -, *, /, %)<a class="heading-anchor" href="#arithmetic-is-standard-mode-" aria-label="Link to this section">#</a></h2>
<p>This is your calculator in its most basic form, standard mode. You have your four classic operations and one sneaky one that trips beginners up every time.</p>
<div class="code-block">
      <div class="code-head">
        <span class="code-lang">JavaScript</span>
        <button type="button" class="code-copy">Copy</button>
      </div>
      <pre class="shiki github-light-high-contrast" tabindex="0"><code><span class="line"><span style="color:#A0111F">let</span><span style="color:#0E1116"> a </span><span style="color:#A0111F">=</span><span style="color:#023B95"> 10</span><span style="color:#0E1116">;</span></span>
<span class="line"><span style="color:#A0111F">let</span><span style="color:#0E1116"> b </span><span style="color:#A0111F">=</span><span style="color:#023B95"> 3</span><span style="color:#0E1116">;</span></span>
<span class="line"></span>
<span class="line"><span style="color:#0E1116">console.</span><span style="color:#622CBC">log</span><span style="color:#0E1116">(a </span><span style="color:#A0111F">+</span><span style="color:#0E1116"> b);  </span><span style="color:#66707B">// 13 → addition</span></span>
<span class="line"><span style="color:#0E1116">console.</span><span style="color:#622CBC">log</span><span style="color:#0E1116">(a </span><span style="color:#A0111F">-</span><span style="color:#0E1116"> b);  </span><span style="color:#66707B">// 7  → subtraction</span></span>
<span class="line"><span style="color:#0E1116">console.</span><span style="color:#622CBC">log</span><span style="color:#0E1116">(a </span><span style="color:#A0111F">*</span><span style="color:#0E1116"> b);  </span><span style="color:#66707B">// 30 → multiplication</span></span>
<span class="line"><span style="color:#0E1116">console.</span><span style="color:#622CBC">log</span><span style="color:#0E1116">(a </span><span style="color:#A0111F">/</span><span style="color:#0E1116"> b);  </span><span style="color:#66707B">// 3.33 → division</span></span>
<span class="line"><span style="color:#0E1116">console.</span><span style="color:#622CBC">log</span><span style="color:#0E1116">(a </span><span style="color:#A0111F">%</span><span style="color:#0E1116"> b);  </span><span style="color:#66707B">// 1  → remainder</span></span></code></pre>
    </div><p>The first four are obvious. But <code>%</code>? Most calculators have this button and most people never touch it. Here&#39;s the secret, it doesn&#39;t give you a percentage. It gives you the leftover remainder after a division.</p>
<blockquote>
<p>🧮 <strong>Calculator Analogy:</strong> Imagine dividing 10 sweets equally among 3 friends. Each gets 3 sweets. But you have <strong>1 left over</strong>. That leftover is exactly what <code>%</code> returns. <code>10 % 3 = 1</code>.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Modulo is surprisingly useful in real code, checking if a number is even or odd, cycling through a list, building a clock that resets to zero, and much more.</p>
<hr>
<h2 id="comparison-is-compare-mode">Comparison Is Compare Mode (==, ===, !=, &gt;, &lt;)<a class="heading-anchor" href="#comparison-is-compare-mode" aria-label="Link to this section">#</a></h2>
<p>Now imagine your calculator has a <strong>compare mode</strong>. Instead of calculating a result, it looks at two values and answers one question: true or false?</p>
<p>Is 10 greater than 5? <strong>True.</strong> Is 3 equal to 7? <strong>False.</strong> That&#39;s exactly what comparison operators do , they always return either <code>true</code> or <code>false</code>, nothing else.</p>
<div class="code-block">
      <div class="code-head">
        <span class="code-lang">JavaScript</span>
        <button type="button" class="code-copy">Copy</button>
      </div>
      <pre class="shiki github-light-high-contrast" tabindex="0"><code><span class="line"><span style="color:#0E1116">console.</span><span style="color:#622CBC">log</span><span style="color:#0E1116">(</span><span style="color:#023B95">10</span><span style="color:#A0111F"> ></span><span style="color:#023B95"> 5</span><span style="color:#0E1116">);   </span><span style="color:#66707B">// true</span></span>
<span class="line"><span style="color:#0E1116">console.</span><span style="color:#622CBC">log</span><span style="color:#0E1116">(</span><span style="color:#023B95">3</span><span style="color:#A0111F"> &#x3C;</span><span style="color:#023B95"> 1</span><span style="color:#0E1116">);    </span><span style="color:#66707B">// false</span></span>
<span class="line"><span style="color:#0E1116">console.</span><span style="color:#622CBC">log</span><span style="color:#0E1116">(</span><span style="color:#023B95">5</span><span style="color:#A0111F"> !=</span><span style="color:#023B95"> 3</span><span style="color:#0E1116">);   </span><span style="color:#66707B">// true (they are NOT equal)</span></span></code></pre>
    </div><p>Now here&#39;s the part that confuses almost every beginner, <code>==</code> versus <code>===</code>. Two equal signs vs three.</p>
<blockquote>
<p>🧮 <strong>Calculator Analogy:</strong> Imagine you type <code>&quot;5&quot;</code> (as text) and <code>5</code> (as a number) into two calculators. They display the same thing on screen. <code>==</code> looks at the screen and says &quot;same!&quot; But <code>===</code> peeks inside the machine and says &quot;one is text, one is a number , they&#39;re NOT the same.&quot;</p>
</blockquote>
<div class="code-block">
      <div class="code-head">
        <span class="code-lang">JavaScript</span>
        <button type="button" class="code-copy">Copy</button>
      </div>
      <pre class="shiki github-light-high-contrast" tabindex="0"><code><span class="line"><span style="color:#0E1116">console.</span><span style="color:#622CBC">log</span><span style="color:#0E1116">(</span><span style="color:#032563">"5"</span><span style="color:#A0111F"> ==</span><span style="color:#023B95"> 5</span><span style="color:#0E1116">);   </span><span style="color:#66707B">// true  ← dangerous!</span></span>
<span class="line"><span style="color:#0E1116">console.</span><span style="color:#622CBC">log</span><span style="color:#0E1116">(</span><span style="color:#032563">"5"</span><span style="color:#A0111F"> ===</span><span style="color:#023B95"> 5</span><span style="color:#0E1116">);  </span><span style="color:#66707B">// false ← correct and safe</span></span></code></pre>
    </div><p>Why is <code>==</code> dangerous, not just loose? Before comparing, it quietly converts both sides to a common type, and the conversion rules produce results nobody would guess:</p>
<div class="code-block">
      <div class="code-head">
        <span class="code-lang">JavaScript</span>
        <button type="button" class="code-copy">Copy</button>
      </div>
      <pre class="shiki github-light-high-contrast" tabindex="0"><code><span class="line"><span style="color:#0E1116">console.</span><span style="color:#622CBC">log</span><span style="color:#0E1116">(</span><span style="color:#023B95">0</span><span style="color:#A0111F"> ==</span><span style="color:#032563"> ""</span><span style="color:#0E1116">);             </span><span style="color:#66707B">// true  ← empty string becomes 0</span></span>
<span class="line"><span style="color:#0E1116">console.</span><span style="color:#622CBC">log</span><span style="color:#0E1116">(</span><span style="color:#023B95">0</span><span style="color:#A0111F"> ==</span><span style="color:#032563"> "0"</span><span style="color:#0E1116">);            </span><span style="color:#66707B">// true</span></span>
<span class="line"><span style="color:#0E1116">console.</span><span style="color:#622CBC">log</span><span style="color:#0E1116">(</span><span style="color:#032563">""</span><span style="color:#A0111F"> ==</span><span style="color:#032563"> "0"</span><span style="color:#0E1116">);           </span><span style="color:#66707B">// false ← but these two aren't equal to each other!</span></span>
<span class="line"><span style="color:#0E1116">console.</span><span style="color:#622CBC">log</span><span style="color:#0E1116">(</span><span style="color:#023B95">false</span><span style="color:#A0111F"> ==</span><span style="color:#032563"> "0"</span><span style="color:#0E1116">);        </span><span style="color:#66707B">// true</span></span>
<span class="line"><span style="color:#0E1116">console.</span><span style="color:#622CBC">log</span><span style="color:#0E1116">(</span><span style="color:#023B95">null</span><span style="color:#A0111F"> ==</span><span style="color:#023B95"> undefined</span><span style="color:#0E1116">);   </span><span style="color:#66707B">// true</span></span>
<span class="line"><span style="color:#0E1116">console.</span><span style="color:#622CBC">log</span><span style="color:#0E1116">([] </span><span style="color:#A0111F">==</span><span style="color:#023B95"> false</span><span style="color:#0E1116">);         </span><span style="color:#66707B">// true  ← an empty array equals false</span></span></code></pre>
    </div><p><code>0</code> equals <code>&quot;&quot;</code> and <code>0</code> equals <code>&quot;0&quot;</code>, but <code>&quot;&quot;</code> doesn&#39;t equal <code>&quot;0&quot;</code>. A comparison you can&#39;t predict is a bug waiting for the right input. Swap <code>==</code> for <code>===</code> and every line above is simply <code>false</code>.</p>
<p>As a rule of thumb, always use <code>===</code> in your code. It checks both the value and the type, so the result is never a surprise.</p>
<hr>
<h2 id="logic-is-decision-mode">Logic Is Decision Mode (&amp;&amp;, ||, !)<a class="heading-anchor" href="#logic-is-decision-mode" aria-label="Link to this section">#</a></h2>
<p>Your calculator can now compare. But what if you need to check <strong>two conditions at the same time</strong>? What if one condition being true is enough? What if you want to flip a result?</p>
<p>That&#39;s logic mode. Logical operators combine true/false values to make bigger decisions.</p>
<blockquote>
<p>🧮 <strong>Calculator Analogy:</strong> Think of a calculator that only turns on if you enter the right PIN <strong>AND</strong> it has battery. Both must be true. But a low-battery warning fires if the battery is low <strong>OR</strong> the charger is unplugged — either one triggers it. And <code>!</code> is the on/off toggle. It flips whatever state you&#39;re in.</p>
</blockquote>
<div class="code-block">
      <div class="code-head">
        <span class="code-lang">JavaScript</span>
        <button type="button" class="code-copy">Copy</button>
      </div>
      <pre class="shiki github-light-high-contrast" tabindex="0"><code><span class="line"><span style="color:#A0111F">let</span><span style="color:#0E1116"> hasBattery </span><span style="color:#A0111F">=</span><span style="color:#023B95"> true</span><span style="color:#0E1116">;</span></span>
<span class="line"><span style="color:#A0111F">let</span><span style="color:#0E1116"> isCharged  </span><span style="color:#A0111F">=</span><span style="color:#023B95"> false</span><span style="color:#0E1116">;</span></span>
<span class="line"></span>
<span class="line"><span style="color:#0E1116">console.</span><span style="color:#622CBC">log</span><span style="color:#0E1116">(hasBattery </span><span style="color:#A0111F">&#x26;&#x26;</span><span style="color:#0E1116"> isCharged);  </span><span style="color:#66707B">// false → both must be true</span></span>
<span class="line"><span style="color:#0E1116">console.</span><span style="color:#622CBC">log</span><span style="color:#0E1116">(hasBattery </span><span style="color:#A0111F">||</span><span style="color:#0E1116"> isCharged);  </span><span style="color:#66707B">// true  → at least one is true</span></span>
<span class="line"><span style="color:#0E1116">console.</span><span style="color:#622CBC">log</span><span style="color:#0E1116">(</span><span style="color:#A0111F">!</span><span style="color:#0E1116">hasBattery);              </span><span style="color:#66707B">// false → flips true to false</span></span></code></pre>
    </div><p>A simple way to remember them:</p>
<ul>
<li><p><code>&amp;&amp;</code> — strict bouncer. <strong>Everyone</strong> in the group must qualify.</p>
</li>
<li><p><code>||</code> — easy bouncer. <strong>Anyone</strong> qualifies, the whole group gets in.</p>
</li>
<li><p><code>!</code> — the invert button. Flips true to false, and false to true.</p>
</li>
</ul>
<hr>
<h2 id="assignment-is-the-memory-button-">Assignment Is the Memory Button (=, +=, -=)<a class="heading-anchor" href="#assignment-is-the-memory-button-" aria-label="Link to this section">#</a></h2>
<p>Every calculator has a <strong>memory button</strong> , M+ to add to memory, MR to recall it. Assignment operators are JavaScript&#39;s memory system. They store values into variables and update them over time.</p>
<div class="code-block">
      <div class="code-head">
        <span class="code-lang">JavaScript</span>
        <button type="button" class="code-copy">Copy</button>
      </div>
      <pre class="shiki github-light-high-contrast" tabindex="0"><code><span class="line"><span style="color:#A0111F">let</span><span style="color:#0E1116"> score </span><span style="color:#A0111F">=</span><span style="color:#023B95"> 0</span><span style="color:#0E1116">;     </span><span style="color:#66707B">// store 0 in memory</span></span>
<span class="line"></span>
<span class="line"><span style="color:#0E1116">score </span><span style="color:#A0111F">+=</span><span style="color:#023B95"> 10</span><span style="color:#0E1116">;       </span><span style="color:#66707B">// same as: score = score + 10 → 10</span></span>
<span class="line"><span style="color:#0E1116">score </span><span style="color:#A0111F">+=</span><span style="color:#023B95"> 5</span><span style="color:#0E1116">;        </span><span style="color:#66707B">// same as: score = score + 5  → 15</span></span>
<span class="line"><span style="color:#0E1116">score </span><span style="color:#A0111F">-=</span><span style="color:#023B95"> 3</span><span style="color:#0E1116">;        </span><span style="color:#66707B">// same as: score = score - 3  → 12</span></span>
<span class="line"></span>
<span class="line"><span style="color:#0E1116">console.</span><span style="color:#622CBC">log</span><span style="color:#0E1116">(score); </span><span style="color:#66707B">// 12</span></span></code></pre>
    </div><blockquote>
<p>🧮 <strong>Calculator Analogy:</strong> When you hit M+ on a calculator, you&#39;re not replacing what&#39;s in memory, you&#39;re <strong>adding to it</strong>. That running total in memory is exactly what <code>+=</code> does to a variable.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>The shorthand operators aren&#39;t just lazy shortcuts — they make your code more readable and less error-prone. <code>score += 10</code> says exactly what you mean in half the characters.</p>
<hr>
<h2 id="putting-it-all-together">Putting It All Together<a class="heading-anchor" href="#putting-it-all-together" aria-label="Link to this section">#</a></h2>
<p>Let&#39;s write a tiny score tracker that uses every operator we&#39;ve covered, just like a real calculator uses all its modes together.</p>
<div class="code-block">
      <div class="code-head">
        <span class="code-lang">JavaScript</span>
        <button type="button" class="code-copy">Copy</button>
      </div>
      <pre class="shiki github-light-high-contrast" tabindex="0"><code><span class="line"><span style="color:#66707B">// Assignment: store starting values</span></span>
<span class="line"><span style="color:#A0111F">let</span><span style="color:#0E1116"> playerScore </span><span style="color:#A0111F">=</span><span style="color:#023B95"> 0</span><span style="color:#0E1116">;</span></span>
<span class="line"><span style="color:#A0111F">let</span><span style="color:#0E1116"> targetScore </span><span style="color:#A0111F">=</span><span style="color:#023B95"> 50</span><span style="color:#0E1116">;</span></span>
<span class="line"><span style="color:#A0111F">let</span><span style="color:#0E1116"> lives </span><span style="color:#A0111F">=</span><span style="color:#023B95"> 3</span><span style="color:#0E1116">;</span></span>
<span class="line"></span>
<span class="line"><span style="color:#66707B">// Arithmetic: calculate points for this round</span></span>
<span class="line"><span style="color:#A0111F">let</span><span style="color:#0E1116"> pointsEarned </span><span style="color:#A0111F">=</span><span style="color:#023B95"> 20</span><span style="color:#A0111F"> +</span><span style="color:#023B95"> 15</span><span style="color:#0E1116">;         </span><span style="color:#66707B">// 35</span></span>
<span class="line"><span style="color:#A0111F">let</span><span style="color:#0E1116"> bonus </span><span style="color:#A0111F">=</span><span style="color:#0E1116"> pointsEarned </span><span style="color:#A0111F">%</span><span style="color:#023B95"> 10</span><span style="color:#0E1116">;      </span><span style="color:#66707B">// 5 (remainder)</span></span>
<span class="line"></span>
<span class="line"><span style="color:#66707B">// Assignment shorthand: update the score</span></span>
<span class="line"><span style="color:#0E1116">playerScore </span><span style="color:#A0111F">+=</span><span style="color:#0E1116"> pointsEarned </span><span style="color:#A0111F">+</span><span style="color:#0E1116"> bonus; </span><span style="color:#66707B">// 0 + 40 = 40</span></span>
<span class="line"></span>
<span class="line"><span style="color:#66707B">// Comparison: has the player won?</span></span>
<span class="line"><span style="color:#A0111F">let</span><span style="color:#0E1116"> hasWon </span><span style="color:#A0111F">=</span><span style="color:#0E1116"> playerScore </span><span style="color:#A0111F">>=</span><span style="color:#0E1116"> targetScore; </span><span style="color:#66707B">// false (40 &#x3C; 50)</span></span>
<span class="line"></span>
<span class="line"><span style="color:#66707B">// Logical: game continues if not won AND has lives left</span></span>
<span class="line"><span style="color:#A0111F">let</span><span style="color:#0E1116"> gameContinues </span><span style="color:#A0111F">=</span><span style="color:#A0111F"> !</span><span style="color:#0E1116">hasWon </span><span style="color:#A0111F">&#x26;&#x26;</span><span style="color:#0E1116"> lives </span><span style="color:#A0111F">></span><span style="color:#023B95"> 0</span><span style="color:#0E1116">; </span><span style="color:#66707B">// true</span></span>
<span class="line"></span>
<span class="line"><span style="color:#0E1116">console.</span><span style="color:#622CBC">log</span><span style="color:#0E1116">(</span><span style="color:#032563">"Score:"</span><span style="color:#0E1116">, playerScore);      </span><span style="color:#66707B">// 40</span></span>
<span class="line"><span style="color:#0E1116">console.</span><span style="color:#622CBC">log</span><span style="color:#0E1116">(</span><span style="color:#032563">"Won?"</span><span style="color:#0E1116">, hasWon);             </span><span style="color:#66707B">// false</span></span>
<span class="line"><span style="color:#0E1116">console.</span><span style="color:#622CBC">log</span><span style="color:#0E1116">(</span><span style="color:#032563">"Continue?"</span><span style="color:#0E1116">, gameContinues); </span><span style="color:#66707B">// true</span></span></code></pre>
    </div><hr>
<h2 id="wrapping-up">Wrapping Up<a class="heading-anchor" href="#wrapping-up" aria-label="Link to this section">#</a></h2>
<p>You&#39;ve been using operators your whole life, in school, on your phone, in your head. JavaScript just gives you a few more modes. Arithmetic crunches the numbers. Comparison checks the results. Logic makes the decision. Assignment remembers it all.</p>
<p>The calculator analogy holds all the way through, with one exception: the <code>==</code> button, which changes what&#39;s on the screen before comparing it. Press <code>===</code> instead, and JavaScript&#39;s calculator behaves exactly like the one you already know.</p>
